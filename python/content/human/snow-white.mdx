Zeus is the canonical master of disguise. No God has disguised themselves so
frequently and so effectively.[^disguises] Zeus' disguises are so famously
convincing that they don't just survive casual inspection, they pass the most
intimate of scrutinies. Zeus has also learned a hard lesson about the need to stay
in disguise — revealing his true form led to the incineration of his lover Semele.
And then he had to have a fetus sewn into his thigh. Not a mistake he's going to
make twice.

[^disguises]: To achieve his (usually lascivious) aims, he has variously disguised
himself as: A white bull, an eagle, an ant, a swan, a cuckoo bird, a serpent, a
shower of gold, a dark mist, a mortal, a goddess, a satyr, and a cupbearer.

So if we don't see among the princesses a lightning bolt wielding, sky ruling, god
fathering character, Zeus is in disguise again, and we can trust it to be a very
good one.

While other gods may be revealed by their domain or iconography, the biggest clue
to Zeus' identity is through his origin story:

- The titan Cronus learns that he is destined to be overcome by his own children, just as he had overthrown his father.
- Cronus tells his wife Rhea to give him Zeus, but Rhea tricks Cronus by giving him a stone to swallow.
- Zeus is sheltered by the Kouretes, the gods of the wild mountainside.
- Zeus returns in disguise and tricks Cronus into drinking a poison, causing Cronus to regurgitate Zeus's siblings.
- After a war, Zeus overthrows Cronus to take his place as the ruler of the gods.

There is exactly one Disney princess story that matches this beat for beat:

- An incumbent ruler is threatened by a successor, which mirrors the manner in which the ruler overthrew the previous ruler.
- The incumbent tasks an agent to kill the successor, but loses heart and instead deceives the ruler with a decoy.
- The successor finds shelter among the wild guardians of the mountains.
- When the successor and incumbent finally meet, one of them is in disguise, and one tricks the other into swallowing a poison.
- The incumbent is vanquished and the successor takes their rightful place on the throne.

The story of Snow White is just a retelling of the Titanomachy, sanitized for
Disney.[^grimm]

[^grimm]: In the original 1812 fairy tale by the Brothers Grimm, *Sneewittchen,*
the parallels are even more explicit. The evil queen eats the decoy lungs and liver
with salt, believing them to be Sneewittchen's.

But where's the war and lightning bolts? Zeus battles Cronus to take his place as
king of the gods. And clearly, Snow White, having just eaten the apple, is out of
action during the entire scene as the dwarfs chase and vanquish the evil queen.

Or is she? Right when Snow White collapses and the evil queen cackles in triumph —
a flash of lightning and the crack of thunder. Torrential rain cascades down while
the evil queen flees the vengeful dwarves. The God of the sky and thunder is close
at hand. Hounded by her pursuers, it seems the evil queen might get away… until
*crack!* A **LIGHTNING BOLT**[^lightningdeath] strikes the ledge she is on and the
queen falls to her death![^castdown]

[^lightningdeath]: Lightning induced death is rare. The only other Disney
characters to die by lightning are Emperor Palpatine (his own lightning, reflected
back at him) and Ursula (debatable, her death is usually attributed to a boat). See
[List of Deaths Wiki](https://listofdeaths.fandom.com/wiki/Disney_Animated_Canon).

[^castdown]: A devil's advocate might say that the evil queen was killed by the
fall, not the lightning bolt. And that for all we know, perhaps she survived the
fall, eh? But that's exactly what happened to Cronus. Cronus wasn't killed by the
lightning bolt. He was cast down into Tartarus, a deep, inescapable abyss beneath
the underworld.
