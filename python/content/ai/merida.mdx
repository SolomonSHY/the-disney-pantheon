Merida introduces herself with an arrow. She rides into her own betrothal
tournament, announces that she will be shooting for her *own* hand, and splits
each suitor's target down the shaft — a girl who would rather vanish into the
Highland wild than be married on someone else's schedule. There is only one
goddess this is a portrait of.

Artemis is the huntress: bow and quiver, the wilderness her domain, the wild
animals her company, and — above all — the fierce, non-negotiable refusal of
marriage that defines her cult. She asked her father for eternal maidenhood
before she asked for anything else. Merida spends her whole film asking for the
same thing in a Scottish accent.

Domain and role sit high — archery, wilderness, the marriage-refuser — and
temperament seals it: independent, headstrong, happiest at a gallop. The gap from
Mulan is small and honest. Merida's rebellion is personal where Athena's Mulan is
civic; the huntress protects her own freedom, the strategist protects the state.
Both draw a bow. Only one is aiming at the wedding itself.
