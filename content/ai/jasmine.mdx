Jasmine's conflict is a law. By the statute of Agrabah she must marry a prince,
and the entire first act is her refusal of that sovereignty-by-marriage — a
princess whose palace is a cage precisely because her value has been fixed to a
wedding. She is royalty defined by the politics of the royal bed, which is the
exact jurisdiction of Hera.

Hera is queen of the gods and goddess of marriage — sovereignty, the palace, and
the marriage-bond fused into one regal, imperious figure. She rules, and she
polices who may wed whom. Jasmine, chafing against a marriage law while radiating
the authority to change it, is a young Hera arguing with the rulebook she will
one day enforce.

> The palace and the marriage law are the same prison and the same throne. She
> wants to keep the throne and burn the law.

In the low twenties, and the reasons are legible. Hera's defining myths are jealous
vengeance against her husband's lovers — a register Jasmine's story never enters.
What matches is the seat of power: sovereignty, wealth, palace, the regal
temperament. She has Hera's crown and station without Hera's grievance.
