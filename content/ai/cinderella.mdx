Her name is an accusation and a hearth: *cinder*, the ash she sleeps beside,
sweeps up, and is named for. Before the ball, before the slipper, Cinderella is
defined entirely by a domestic order she maintains without reward — the fire
kept, the floors scrubbed, the household running on her invisible labour. She is
the tender of a home that refuses to call her family.

There is exactly one Olympian who *is* that: Hestia, eldest of the gods, goddess
of the hearth, who gave up her throne to keep the sacred fire and asked for
nothing in return. She has no myths of her own because she never leaves the
centre of the house. Neither, until midnight, does Cinderella.

The fit is almost total on domain and temperament — the patient, self-effacing
keeper of the flame — and it is total on the thing the other rankings miss:
Cinderella's virtue is not passivity but *constancy*. She does the work whether
or not anyone is watching, which is the whole theology of the hearth.

Where she and Hestia part is arc. Hestia never changes; that is her power.
Cinderella does leave the ashes — and the story only works because we know how
much it costs the goddess of staying to finally go.
