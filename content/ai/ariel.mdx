Ariel wants to cross over, and she is willing to pay for it in her own voice. Her
story is transgression as appetite: a creature of one world who trades her body
and her song to enter another, defying her father, striking a bargain with a
witch, dissolving the boundary between sea and shore through sheer desire. She is
not the goddess of the sea. She is the impulse that breaks its rules.

That impulse is Dionysus — god of liberation, transformation, and ecstatic
boundary-crossing, the outsider deity who dissolves the line between states and
liberates through transgression. Ariel's metamorphosis from fins to legs is a
Dionysian rite: the self remade by wanting, order overturned by longing.

The low-twenties score is a story about mismatched *keys*, not mismatched themes.
Dionysus is also wine, madness, and revelry — a riot Ariel's teenage yearning only
distantly resembles. What connects them is the deep structure: liberation, the
crossing, the transformation of the body to satisfy desire. She is Dionysus with
the wine swapped for a crush, and the ecstasy pointed entirely at a pair of legs.
