Belle is the bride of a monster, and the beauty in the title is hers. She is sent
to a dark house to love something frightening and, by loving it, to break its
curse and transform it — a story so old it has a name older than Disney: Cupid and
Psyche, the mortal girl wed to a hidden, monstrous bridegroom she must learn to
see.

Which is the honest problem with this chapter. Belle's true analogue is *Psyche*
— but Psyche is no Olympian, and this pantheon has only twelve seats and a handful
of alternates. Aphrodite is the nearest available shelf: goddess of beauty, love,
and desire, the deity whose whole domain is the force that finally frees the
Beast. In the Psyche myth, Aphrodite is the antagonist. Here she stands in for the
girl she tormented.

So the low score of twenty is a measure of *substitution error* as much as of fit.
Belle has Aphrodite's domain — beauty, love, the transforming power of desire —
and almost none of her vanity or caprice. The book, the rose, the transforming
love: those match. The goddess herself is a placeholder for a myth this pantheon
can't quite hold.
