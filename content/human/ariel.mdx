This brings us to the last pairing: Dionysus as Ariel. It isn't a sacrificial
match, and we didn't reach it by elimination — it's a high-confidence match from
the profile matrix and the Hungarian algorithm.

Dionysus is the god of wine, orchards, fruit, vegetation, fertility, festivity,
insanity, ritual madness, ecstasy, and theatre. Unlike Artemis, who rules the
wilds, Dionysus exists specifically to *subvert authority* and liberate his
followers from social convention. He is also a god of foreign origin — the
arriving outsider-god.

## Ariel, the perfect stranger

Ashore, Ariel is the embodiment of the outsider. No one knows where she came
from, who she is, or how she got into Prince Eric's kingdom. She doesn't speak a
word — she has to mime theatrically to get anything across. At dinner in the
castle she is utterly unfamiliar with human custom: she drinks confidently from a
finger bowl, styles her hair with a fork, believes a smoking pipe is a musical
instrument, and seizes the carriage reins to drive at reckless speed. Ungoverned
at the table, exuberant where restraint is expected — the exact shape of Dionysus.

Dionysus is **twice-born.** In the Orphic myth his old body is destroyed and he
is reborn in a new one. Unlike Tiana, who returns to her original form, Ariel
ends her film in a *new body* — on two legs.

But where's the wine? No wine, no Dionysus, period — I don't care if it's a
G-rated movie. Dionysian wine and ecstasy free followers from fear and subvert
the restraints of the powerful, so if it's anywhere, it's at the moment of
transformation. Zoom in: a clandestine meeting, frowned on by right-thinking
merfolk; Ariel here on impulse, in defiance of King Triton. Something's brewing —
literally, as Ursula throws ingredients into a bubbling cauldron, a concoction
"for poor unfortunate souls." That's an on-the-nose way of saying *drugs or
alcohol.* It promises happiness and freedom at a cost: she surrenders the organ
of self-expression — her voice, which is also how Eric would recognise her.
Dionysian ecstasy dissolves the boundary of the self.

In the film, smoke leaps from the cauldron and seizes her — a "brew" that never
goes near her mouth. To find the actual drink I had to go back to Hans Christian
Andersen's 1837 *Den lille Havfrue:*

> The little mermaid swallowed the bitter, fiery draught, and it was as if a
> two-edged sword struck through her frail body; she swooned away and lay there
> as if dead.

There's the wine. And it is strong stuff — one might almost say it is 100% proof.

**Extra-diegetic.** In his founding myth Dionysus nearly died: his mortal mother
Semele was incinerated by Zeus's true form, and Zeus sewed the unborn child into
his own thigh — the "twice-born god." By 1985, Disney animation was effectively
dead; *The Black Cauldron* had bombed so badly the studio considered shuttering
animation entirely, thirty years since the last princess. Then, in 1989, a girl
with a stolen voice came up out of the water, and *The Little Mermaid* began the
Disney Renaissance. The studio was born a second time.
