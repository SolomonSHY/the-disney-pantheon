This brings us to our last pairing, Dionysus as Ariel. It's not a sacrificial
pairing, and we didn't get here via a process of elimination. This is a high
confidence match derived by our profile matrix and the Hungarian algorithm, and
here's why.

Dionysus is the god of wine-making, orchards, fruit, vegetation, fertility,
festivity, insanity, ritual madness, religious ecstasy, and theatre. Unlike
Artemis, who rules the wilds, Dionysus specifically exists to subvert authority
and liberate his followers from social convention.

In Greek mythology, Dionysus is a god of foreign origin — the arriving
outsider-god. Ariel exhibits obsessive xenophilia through the treasures she has
collected. Ashore, Ariel is the perfect embodiment of the stranger. No one knows
where she came from, who she is, why she is in Prince Eric's kingdom or how she
got there. She doesn't speak a word. She has to mime theatrically to get any
message across.

When Ariel dines with Prince Eric in his castle, she is completely unfamiliar with
human customs. Mistaking a fancy porcelain finger bowl filled with warm water for
a soup or tea cup, she raises it up and takes a confident drink from it, much to
the confusion of Grimsby. She believes a common fork is used to style hair. She
believes a smoking pipe is a musical instrument. She seizes the reins during the
carriage ride and drives at reckless speed. She's ungoverned at the table,
exuberant where restraint is expected. She's exuberant and unrestrained, the exact
shape of Dionysus.

Dionysus is twice born. In the Orphic myth, Dionysus' old body was destroyed and
he needed to be born in a new one. Unlike Tiana, who returns to her original form,
Ariel ends her movie in a new body — on two legs.

## But where's the wine?

Dionysus is the god of wine. No wine, no Dionysus, period. I don't care if it's a
G-rated Disney movie.

Where would the wine be? Dionysus' wine, music, and ecstatic dance were considered
to free his followers from self-conscious fear and care, and subvert the
oppressive restraints of the powerful. Wine could ease suffering, bring joy, and
inspire divine madness. Well, if anywhere, it has to be at the point of Ariel's
transformation.

Let's zoom in on that scene. It's a clandestine meeting frowned upon by any right
thinking merfolk. Ariel is here on impulse, in direct defiance of King Triton,
which sets the scene perfectly. The festivals of Dionysus are subversive and
transgressed traditional social and moral constraints.

Something's brewing. Literally, as Ursula throws magical ingredients into a
bubbling, steaming cauldron — a concoction for poor unfortunate souls who are
miserable, lonely and depressed. That's pretty on-the-nose way of saying: it's
drugs or alcohol. It promises Ariel happiness and freedom… at a cost. She
surrenders the organ of self-expression — her voice. And it isn't just
self-expression: her voice is how Prince Eric recognizes her so she's also trading
her identity. Dionysian ecstasy dissolves the boundary of the self.

The scene escalates. There's smoke and flashes of light, like fireworks at a
festival. Ursula's singing and dancing. Ariel sings. Now Ursula laughs madly.
Drink the magic juice. But she doesn't drink it! In the Disney movie, smoke leaps
from the cauldron and seizes Ariel, completing the transformation. What is so
obviously a brew doesn't even act like a liquid, or go anywhere near her mouth.

To actually find the brew, I had to go to a story written in 1837 by Hans
Christian Andersen, *Den lille Havfrue,* and found this banger of a line: *"Den
lille havfrue drak den brændende skarpe drik, og det var, som gik et tveægget
sværd igennem hendes fine legeme, hun besvimede derved og lå, som død."*

Or, in English: *"The little mermaid swallowed the bitter, fiery draught, and it
was as if a two-edged sword struck through her frail body; she swooned away and
lay there as if dead."*

There's the wine. And it is strong stuff. One might almost say it is 100% proof.
